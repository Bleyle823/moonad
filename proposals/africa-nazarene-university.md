MOONAD is a small rover that does paid work on the Moon, and shares what it earns with the people on Earth who helped pay for it. It surveys ground, finds a sample, and brings that sample back to a landing pad. A buyer pays when the job is confirmed. The rover can also pay a neighbouring machine for power or a radio link. The people who funded it receive their share of what the rover earns.

The rover in the project today lives in a computer model of the lunar surface. There is also a Mars landscape for the same kind of drive. A rover on the Moon is a later step, after a ground test and a ride with a licensed mission. The milestone to earn is a rover whose work and payment can be checked. That milestone is still ahead.

## What a job is

The rover has six wheels and can steer. In the simulation it plans a path around rocks and craters, slows down when the ground ahead is hard to read, stops if something is close, and drives on. It looks for a sample, then returns to the pad.

A job has three marks: the rover came online, it found a sample, and it delivered that sample. Those marks are written into a log on the machine. Later, that log is what a buyer checks before paying. The rover drives itself. A message from Earth to the Moon takes more than a second, so the wheels cannot wait for a person, or for a payment network, to decide each turn.

Three rules sit above the demo:

- The rover does the driving.
- Payment follows a confirmed job.
- Earnings, when they exist, are shared with the people who funded the rover. Those shares are set with them first. This letter leaves the percentages blank.

## Why this matters now

Kenya already treats space as a national skill, not a distant hobby.

The Kenya Space Agency, established in 2017, is mandated to promote, coordinate, and regulate space activities so the country can use them for development. Kenya has built and flown small satellites, including 1KUNS-PF and Taifa-1. In February 2025 the Agency and Multimedia University of Kenya trained students on CubeSats. In January 2026 the Agency joined an Earth-observation hackathon at the University of Nairobi, where student teams built short prototypes from satellite data.

MOONAD sits in that training line as a graduate project: a machine students can watch do a job, with a plain rule for how the job gets paid. The Agency has not adopted this project. A class at Africa Nazarene University can still open the simulation and see a rover work.

The aims are these:

- A demonstration a student can follow: drive, find, deliver, record.
- A design in which a buyer pays for a confirmed job, and funders on Earth share the earnings.
- A later ground test on a bed of imitation lunar soil, then a conversation with a licensed mission. No date is promised.
- Kenyan graduates who can explain a space machine without pretending it has already landed.

These are aims. The rover has not left the simulation.

## How the record fits

The lunar landscape on screen is a simulation. The rover writes a local log: online, travelling, sample found, sample delivered. peaq is the network where that log is meant to become a public record, tied to the rover's identity, so a buyer can check the job before paying. That public step is next. The simulation does not yet send a live transaction.

Co-ownership is the other half. People on Earth who fund the rover would hold a share of what it earns. The share moves when a job is paid. Selling those shares to the public waits on legal advice. The demonstration can show the split on a test record first.

The wheels stay with the rover. The public record is the receipt.

## What is real today

The simulation runs. The rover can drive on a model of the Moon, look for a sample, and write the trip into a log. A public identity, a live payment, and a rover on the Moon are the steps after this one.
